# Getting adopted

Use this when a specific group already wants the product and the question is how it spreads, or why it stalls after the first win. Market choice, including Moore’s beachhead, is in [Where to play](where-to-play.md). Do that first. The books here are what you do after the field is chosen.

## After the beachhead

Moore’s sequence (chasm, then tornado) says the mainstream buys a whole product and a reference from their own peers, and that a market in tornado rewards the standard and distribution. The implication for this file is narrow and strict: **channel work before a beachhead teaches you the average of several markets, which is a market you do not have.** Ellis and Brown (*Hacking Growth*) are the right tempo once a defined segment retains because it got the core value. Their machinery is a growth team, a named aha moment, a high rate of experiments, and the pirate metrics (acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue) as a diagnostic, not as five goals. If retention of the beachhead is flat, acquisition experiments burn money. Ellis’s North Star, in [How the team works](how-the-team-works.md), is the check: a growth program that does not move core value is a louder funnel.

Weinberg and Mares (*Traction*) agree that traction is half the company, not a coat of paint at the end, and then refuse the conclusion that you should dabble in every channel. Their bullseye narrows a long list of channels to the one that can work now, and kills the others on purpose. Fifty percent of the effort on traction means the channel is a product problem (does the product carry the channel), not a task for a separate team after launch.

## The other book called Traction

Cleveland’s *Traversing the Traction Gap* is not Weinberg. The file’s metadata names Cleveland and that title; the body did not extract, so the note below is the book’s published spine, not a page from the PDF. He describes company stages and a gap in the middle: you have a product, you do not yet have repeatable traction, and that gap is where startups die while looking busy. Weinberg answers "which channel." Cleveland answers "which stage, and what has to be true before you scale spend." Cite the surname. "The traction book" is how these two get blended into advice that is stronger than either one.

## Motions, networks, and launches

GTM Partners’ guide: most companies have more than one go-to-market type, and each type has its own economics. Inbound (content into a conversion path), outbound (named accounts), product-led, and marketplace motions do not share a playbook. Spending inbound effort on a niche that can only be won outbound raises the cost to acquire a customer. Name the motion per segment. A single bullseye channel is right when there is one motion. It is wrong when sales and product-led are both real and you pretend one dashboard covers them.

Chen (*The Cold Start Problem*) is the exception that swallows ordinary funnels. A network product is worthless to the first user. The work is an atomic network small enough to be dense, a hard side you solve first, often a tool that is useful alone so people stay long enough for the network to matter, then a tipping point, then the problems of escape velocity, then a ceiling when the network is large. A North Star of "signups" is the wrong star. A growth experiment that adds users to an empty network makes the product look worse.

Product School’s *Launch* pamphlet treats launch as the work of getting a person through first use, not as a date. That is the part to keep. A tour glued on top of a control that Norman would call a design error is the part to refuse; that test is in [What to build](what-to-build.md). Heath and Heath on peaks and pits (*The Power of Moments*) matter here only as a launch check: the first session is a transition, and people remember the peak and the pit more than the average screen. The rest of that book is in [The person and the room](the-person-and-the-room.md).

The Product Analytics Economy pamphlet (Product School with Heap) includes a shift into product-led growth. Use it as a motion in the GTM list above. Its warning that analytics can be made useless belongs in [Judgment](judgment.md), next to Heap’s own questions book.

## Habits, points, and nudges

Eyal (*Hooked*, and the notes file, which is the same argument) describes a loop: trigger, action, variable reward, investment. External triggers (a ping, an email) start the loop. Internal triggers (boredom, fear, loneliness) are what make it a habit. The action has to be easier than the motivation you can count on. The notes file is explicit: when you are choosing whether to push motivation or ability, start with ability. Variable reward keeps the search alive. Investment (data, reputation, skill, followers) loads the next trigger and makes leaving costly. Eyal’s ethical test is whether you would use the product yourself and whether it materially improves the user’s life. Hold him to it.

Hyzy and Wardle (*Gamification for Product Excellence*) offer the engagement layer: progress, status, rewards, loops aimed at retention. Use that layer on an action the person already has a reason to do, because the job is real. A points system on a product that failed the Mom Test hides the failure for a quarter.

Thaler and Sunstein (*Nudge*) set the ethical line used in [Judgment](judgment.md): you are always designing the choice, the person should be able to opt out cheaply, and the arrangement should serve what they would want if they were paying attention. A variable reward that exploits an internal trigger the user would rather be free of fails that line even when the loop is elegant. So does a default that is only good for the seller.

Incentives can also eat the behavior you wanted. If the only reason to do the core action is the point, removing the point removes the action, and you have trained people to ignore the job. Klement’s forces in [What to build](what-to-build.md) are the check: habit of the present and anxiety about the new way are not the same thing as a badge.

## A plan for a product that already works for someone

1. Write the beachhead in one sentence. If you cannot, go to [Where to play](where-to-play.md) and stop channel work.
2. Name the motion for that segment: inbound, outbound, product-led, or a network’s atomic starting point.
3. If it is a network, define the smallest dense side. Ignore signup targets until that side is dense.
4. If it is not, run Weinberg’s narrowing: one channel, a kill criterion, product changes the channel requires.
5. Define the aha and the retention read. No acquisition scale while retention of that segment is unexplained.
6. Draw the habit loop only if the action is one the user would want when calm. If the loop depends on them not noticing, do not ship it.
7. Read launch as the first session. Remove tours that compensate for a control the product should explain.
