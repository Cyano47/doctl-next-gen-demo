# Where to play

Use this when the argument is no longer "can we make this feature" and is "why this, for this group, instead of everything else." Discovery tests a bet. Strategy chooses which bets are even allowed.

**Paired with:** how to test a chosen bet is in [What to build](what-to-build.md). Goals, OKRs, and roadmaps as a management system are in [How the team works](how-the-team-works.md). Channels come after the market choice; that rule is in [Getting adopted](getting-adopted.md).

## What counts as a strategy

Rumelt (*Good Strategy/Bad Strategy*) is the test the other books have to pass. A strategy has a kernel:

1. **Diagnosis.** What is actually going on, and which part of it is the crux.
2. **Guiding policy.** The approach that deals with the crux. This is a choice, including what you will not do.
3. **Coherent actions.** Steps that reinforce each other. A list of initiatives that could belong to any company fails here.

Bad strategy, in his account, looks like ambition dressed up as a plan: fluff, a goal with no diagnosis, and objectives that do not constrain anyone. A proximate objective is close enough that the organization can actually reach it. "Be the leading platform" is not proximate.

Write the kernel in a page before you adopt anyone else’s framework below. If you cannot diagnose, a category narrative or an OKR will fill the hole with confidence.

## Choosing the field

**A secret and a small monopoly.** Thiel (*Zero to One*, with Masters) separates vertical progress (zero to one, something new) from horizontal progress (one to n, copying). Competition, in this argument, competes profits away. The company worth building holds a secret: something true and important that others do not see or will not say. Start by monopolizing a tiny market, then expand. Definite optimism means you have a plan you are willing to state. It is a direct argument against treating the future as something you only discover by experimenting in every direction.

**A category you name.** Ramadan, Peterson, Lochhead, and Maney (*Play Bigger*) argue that the company which defines the problem owns the category. The work is a point of view, a language customers use, and a moment (their lightning strike) when the market hears the new frame. This is how you behave when the existing category hides the problem you solve.

**A slot in a mind that is already full.** Ries and Trout (*Positioning*) start from the opposite observation: the society is overcommunicated, and more explanation does not land. You win a position that already has a ladder in the customer’s head, often by being first, by relating to the leader, or by specializing. You do not win by describing every feature. Jiwa (*Meaningful*) is about how that position gets said; the writing craft is in [The person and the room](the-person-and-the-room.md).

**A reconstructed offer, not a better one.** Kim and Mauborgne (the Blue Ocean Strategy reader in the folder) argue for value innovation: a strategy canvas of how the industry competes, then four moves — eliminate, reduce, raise, create — so you stop matching rivals factor by factor. The file is a reader, so treat the canvas and the four moves as the load-bearing ideas, and go back to the full book before you run a large process on excerpts.

**A niche on the adoption curve, in order.** Moore (*Crossing the Chasm*, and *Inside the Tornado*) splits buyers. Visionaries buy a promise. The early majority buys a whole product and a reference from someone like them. The chasm is the gap between those groups. The move is a beachhead: one narrow segment you can dominate, a whole product for that segment, then adjacent segments (the bowling alley). *Inside the Tornado* is the later phase: when the mainstream tips, the market wants the safe standard, and distribution beats elegance. Custom work that delighted visionaries becomes a trap in the tornado. Both PDF copies of *Crossing the Chasm* are the same book.

**The incumbent’s trap.** Christensen (*The Innovator’s Dilemma*) argues that well-run incumbents fail by listening to their best customers and protecting their margins. Disruptive offers start worse on the dimensions those customers care about, and better on dimensions those customers do not (simpler, cheaper, a new context). The rational resource allocation of the core business starves the disruption. His structural answer is a separate organization with its own cost structure. This is the same author as *Competing Against Luck*, doing a different job: that book is about why a person switches; this one is about why a successful firm will not.

**Discipline after a series of wins.** Collins (*Good to Great*) argues that the companies in his study got a flywheel turning: disciplined people, disciplined thought, disciplined action; confront the brutal facts; a hedgehog that is the overlap of what you can be best at, what drives the economic engine, and what you care about deeply; level 5 leaders who combine humility with will. Use the brutal-facts question and the "stop doing" list. Do not use the study as a method that produces greatness. Several of the companies later fell apart, and Rumelt’s standard is a mechanism you can choose, not a portrait of winners after the fact.

## Where they disagree

### Commit to a secret, or test your way there

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Thiel | The question is whether this should exist as a company. If there is no secret and no path to a small monopoly, more experiments produce a feature-rich copy. |
| Ries, in [What to build](what-to-build.md) | You already have a point of view and the unknown is a specific assumption that could kill it. Experiment there. |
| Rumelt | You need to see whether the "secret" is actually a diagnosis. A secret you cannot connect to coherent actions is a vision slide. |

Thiel is not a license to skip customer evidence. He is a license to refuse a roadmap that is only a tour of competitors’ features.

### Niche, new category, or a wider market

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Moore | You have a technology the enthusiasts love and the mainstream does not trust. Pick one beachhead and build the whole product. Refuse the demand to sell to everyone. |
| Ries and Trout | The category is already understood. Claim a position the mind can store. More education will not create a new ladder. |
| Play Bigger | The old category is why buyers cannot see you. The work is language and a point of view, timed as a strike, not a slow drip of feature announcements. |
| Kim and Mauborgne | You are matching rivals on the same factors and the margin for being "better" is gone. Reconstruct the factors. |
| Thiel | The niche is not a messaging choice. It is the market you monopolize first. |

These compose in time. A new category (Play Bigger, blue ocean) still has to become the safe choice for pragmatists (Moore) or it remains a visionary toy. A position (Ries and Trout) still has to be a monopoly somewhere (Thiel) or it is a slogan with no economics. Do the kernel first so you know which of these problems you actually have.

### Listen to your best customers

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Christensen, *The Innovator’s Dilemma* | Your best customers and your margin rules are pulling you up-market, and the threat looks like a toy. House that work where the core P&L cannot starve it. |
| Collins | The failure mode is undisciplined expansion: new bets that are not the hedgehog, facts nobody will say out loud. |
| Moore | Your best current buyers may be visionaries. Their requests are the wrong spec for the majority. |
| The discovery books | "Listen" means study the struggle. It does not mean ship the feature the largest account asked for. That distinction is in [What to build](what-to-build.md). |

### A goal is not a strategy

Doerr’s OKR system is a cadence for focus and grading. It is documented in [How the team works](how-the-team-works.md). Rumelt’s rule belongs here: an objective, however ambitious, is not a diagnosis and not a guiding policy. If the strategy doc is a stack of KRs, send it back. Write the kernel, then let the KRs measure whether the guiding policy is working.

Collins’s hedgehog fails the same test when it is a slogan ("we are about customer obsession") with no crux and no coherent actions.

## A page you can write

1. Diagnosis in five sentences. What changed, who is stuck, what the crux is.
2. Guiding policy in three sentences. Include one thing you will stop.
3. Name which field-choice above you are actually using, and why the others are the wrong problem this year.
4. Three coherent actions that would be irrational if the diagnosis were false.
5. The disconfirming evidence. If you cannot imagine evidence against the strategy, it is a goal.
