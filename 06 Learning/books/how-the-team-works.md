# How the team works

Use this when the fight is about the job, the roadmap, the goals, or who is allowed to say no. The discovery methods themselves are in [What to build](what-to-build.md). The strategy kernel those goals have to serve is in [Where to play](where-to-play.md).

## What the job is

The folder contains two incompatible job descriptions. Both are real. They describe different amounts of power.

**The team is hired to produce an outcome.** Cagan (*INSPIRED*, *EMPOWERED*) draws feature teams and empowered product teams. A feature team receives a roadmap of outputs. An empowered team receives a problem and is accountable for the outcome. The product manager is responsible for value and business viability, the designer for usability, the tech lead for feasibility. Leaders earn that setup by staffing senior people, coaching, setting strategy, and refusing to hand down the solution. Perri (*Escaping the Build Trap*) names the same failure as a company disease: success is counted as things shipped (output) rather than behavior changed (outcome) or the business result (impact). Strategy is a set of bets deployed into teams, not a project list. A product kata — direction, current state, next step, what you learned — replaces the annual roadmap as the learning loop. Fund products and teams, not one-off projects, or the team will be reassembled every quarter and will never hold the outcome.

Banfield, Eriksson, and Walkingshaw (*Product Leadership*) are useful mostly as history: the title has meant outbound marketing, a technical spec owner, a program manager, and a project manager, often all called "PM." The leader’s job in their account is the team and the system. Anon and González de Villaumbrosia (*The Product Book*) open on Ken Norton’s line that nobody asked the PM to show up: engineering builds, design shapes the experience, marketing and sales move it. The seat exists to hold the "why" across those groups. Product School’s pamphlets in the folder (*Ship It*, and both *Product Mindset* files) say the same thing at orientation depth: know the customer, ship, there is a path. Use them in a first week. Do not use them to settle an argument with Cagan or Perri.

**The job on an ordinary Tuesday is connective.** LeMay (*Product Management in Practice*) is the correction when the company has not actually granted the power Cagan describes. His CORE frame is communication, organization, research, and execution. You are not the CEO of the product. You often do not control the people whose work you need. Treating a feature-factory job as an empowered-team job produces shame and bad OKRs. Bavaro and McDowell (*Cracking the PM Career*) are closer to this craft than their interview book is: ladders, org excellence, the skills to operate. Use the career book to see the work. Use the interview book only to see the test.

Productboard and Prodify (*10 Dysfunctions of Product Management*) open on the paradox rather than a clean definition: customer or business, art or science, priorities for now or a longer plan, full accountability without full authority. The rest of that ebook did not yield a list worth reconstructing. The paradox of accountability without authority is LeMay’s Tuesday and Cagan’s design bug. Either give the team the problem and the room to solve it, or be honest that the PM’s job is alignment inside someone else’s roadmap. Writing outcome OKRs on top of a handed-down feature list pretends the first situation while paying people for the second.

## How work is chosen

Adzic (*Impact Mapping*): the center of the map is why. Then who must change, then how their behavior changes, then what you would build. He notes that people doing the work often do not know the business objective, and that a vague goal is not knowledge of why. If a stakeholder request cannot be hung on an impact, it waits.

**North Star** (Amplitude’s playbook, using Sean Ellis’s definition): one metric that captures the core value customers get, plus a handful of inputs the team can actually move. The combination is there so decisions can be made without waiting for a central queue, so teams align, and so daily work stays connected to value. Revenue is a poor North Star. It lags, and many teams can move it by harming the value (discounts, dark patterns, spend). The inputs are where a team’s week shows up.

**OKRs** (Doerr, *Measure What Matters*, from Grove’s Intel practice): an objective is what you want, key results are how you know. They are public. They are graded. The point is focus and a cadence, not a dashboard of everything. Grove’s contribution is the management logic under that: a manager’s output is the output of their own organization plus the output of the neighboring organizations they influence. Task-relevant maturity decides the style. Someone who has never done the task gets a concrete instruction. Someone who has done it gets the outcome and the context. One-on-ones are the tool. Indicators exist so you are not managing from anecdotes.

**Impact, North Star, OKR.** Use them as one stack, not three religions.

| Layer | Holds | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strategy kernel | Diagnosis and guiding policy. [Where to play](where-to-play.md). | Replaced by a pile of objectives. |
| North Star and inputs | The value you exist to deliver, and the few numbers that produce it. | The star is revenue, or every team picks a star that cannot add up. |
| OKRs for the cycle | The change you are committing to now, graded. | The key result is "ship X." That is Perri’s output, with a number painted on it. |
| Impact map | The specific behavior change that would move an input. | The deliverable was chosen first and the impact was invented afterward. |

Grove’s task-relevant maturity and Cagan’s empowerment agree about senior people and disagree about novices. A new PM should be told how discovery works here. They should not be told which feature to specify. "Coach everyone and hand down nothing" abandons the novice. "Specify the solution for everyone" is the build trap.

## Ops, agile, and the backlog

Dragonboat’s *Product Ops Playbook* gives ops a mission: an outcome-driven culture, a portfolio you can actually see, and three pillars — make teams faster and more consistent, align top-down goals with bottom-up bets, orchestrate work across teams. The activities are tooling, planning cadence, stakeholder visibility, research and customer access, analytics, launch planning, and headcount. That is the useful version.

The failure mode is ops as a second product manager. If ops owns the priority, the empowered team is a slide. Ops should make evidence, release, and portfolio state easy to see. The team still chooses the next bet inside the strategy.

PMI’s *Agile Practice Guide* says agile spread because organizations want to change course, and it also says predictive planning still fits some work. Use adaptive delivery when the solution is still moving. Use a plan when the problem and the solution are both known. Agile does not choose the problem. A two-week cadence on a feature list handed down from above is the build trap at a higher tempo. Perri’s test is the one to apply: did the sprint change a behavior you named in advance, or did it burn a queue.

## Interviews and casebooks

McDowell and Bavaro (*Cracking the PM Interview*), Lin (*Decode and Conquer*), and Lin and Lu (*The Product Manager Interview*) teach a performance: a structured answer to a hypothetical product question, with a framework the interviewer can follow. The IIMA casebook and the IIMB Sigma casebook are campus drill books for that performance. Practice there if you have that exam. Do not import their picture of the job over LeMay, Perri, and Cagan.

The contradiction is the point. Interview loops reward a fluent recommendation from thin facts. Torres, Fitzpatrick, and Savoia reward evidence and a bet you could lose. A brilliant CIRCLES-style answer that you would not fund is a pass on the test and a fail on the work. When you hire, ask for a screen of how they actually decided a real bet, which is the move in the company-OS note, not another hypothetical.

## A check before the quarterly plan

1. Is this team handed a problem or a feature list? Say which, in the plan, in one sentence.
2. Write the North Star and at most three inputs. If you cannot, you are not ready for OKRs.
3. Every key result is a behavior or a business result. Rewrite any KR that is a launch.
4. Hang the launches you still believe in on an impact map. Drop the ones that do not change an impact.
5. Match the management style to task-relevant maturity. Name who gets coaching and who still needs a concrete method.
6. Confirm ops is not the shadow prioritizer.
