# The person and the room

Use this when the product argument is fine and the work is still stuck on a manager, a negotiation, a write-up, or a culture that cannot hear bad news. Cognitive biases and "is this number real" stay in [Judgment](judgment.md). Grove’s one-on-ones and task-relevant maturity stay in [How the team works](how-the-team-works.md), because they are part of the operating system, not a leadership style you layer on later.

## When there is no clean recipe

Horowitz (*The Hard Thing About Hard Things*) is the book for the week the framework does not fit. The struggle is the job: layoffs, demotions, politics, training the people you have because the people you wish you had are not on the market. He separates peacetime (expand, tolerate disagreement, build culture) from wartime (one priority, tighter command, survival). Most "how great companies work" advice is peacetime advice delivered into a wartime week.

Collins’s level 5 portrait — humility plus will — is developed in [Where to play](where-to-play.md) as part of a study of companies, not as a method. It is a poor manual for Horowitz’s week. You can be humble and still owe the company a decision today that injures someone. Use Collins to ask whether success has made you undisciplined. Use Horowitz when you need to do the ugly thing and then live with it. Neither book is permission to skip the diagnosis Rumelt asks for.

Catmull (*Creativity, Inc.*, with Wallace) is about protecting new work without lying to yourself. The Braintrust at Pixar reviews the film in progress with candor and without authority: the director still owns the picture. Early ideas are ugly and easy to kill with notes that sound smart. Success hides problems, so a win is when you go looking for what the win is concealing. That pairs with Cagan’s empowered team: advice can be sharp, ownership stays with the people who hold the outcome. It also has a phase limit from Moore. Early, protect the ugly version. In a mainstream tornado, the market wants the standard, and endless protection of every special case becomes the trap *Inside the Tornado* describes. Phase tells you which book is in force.

Goleman (*Emotional Intelligence*) names the skills around that candor: knowing what you feel, regulating it, using it as motivation, reading the other person, handling the social situation. The claim that emotional skill outranks technical skill has been stretched far past the evidence. Competence still gates the conversation. A candid Braintrust of people who do not know the craft is just a loud room. Grove’s maturity scale is the check on whether empathy is enough this quarter.

Warikoo (*Get Epic Shit Done*) is personal advice — saying no, asking for help, boundaries, habits, anger, family — from a book that admits it is full of prescriptions. It is not a product system. Where it touches the job, it touches time and refusal: a manager who cannot say no will accept every stakeholder impact and destroy the strategy kernel. Duke would call the prescriptions untested for your case. Use a chapter as a prompt. Do not install the book as an operating system.

## The bargain and the conversation

Fisher, Ury, and Patton (*Getting to Yes*; no extractable text, so this is that book’s published method) separate the invention of the deal from the tug-of-war. People are not the problem. Interests sit under positions. Invent options before you converge. Insist on a criterion outside either side’s will. Know your BATNA, the walkaway you actually have, before you sit down. Splitting the difference is a reflex, not a principle. It is acceptable when the midpoint matches a criterion you would defend. It is a failure when it is how you avoid naming one.

Voss (*Never Split the Difference*, with Raz; no extractable text, same caveat) argues that the Harvard method assumes a counterpart calm enough to problem-solve. Scared or angry people do not start there. His moves are tactical empathy, labels ("it seems like…"), mirrors, an accusation audit that names their negative view first, calibrated questions that start with how or what, and "that’s right" as the sign they feel understood. "You’re right" is a brush-off.

Use both, in order.

| Moment | Follow |
| --- | --- |
| Preparing the deal | Fisher. Interests, several options, a criterion, a real walkaway. |
| In the room, once emotion is the blocker | Voss. Labels and calibrated questions until the other side can think again. |
| Closing | Fisher’s criterion. Split only if the split is that criterion. Voss’s title is the warning against splitting in order to escape discomfort. |

A roadmap negotiation is this table. The position is "we need the feature in Q1." The interest might be a renewal, a fear of looking unprepared, or a dependency. The criterion might be the impact map. If you split by slipping half the scope with no criterion, you have done the thing both books, for different reasons, tell you to examine.

## Saying it so it survives

Zinsser (*On Writing Well*): clutter is the main defect. Prefer simple words, a human voice, and another pass that cuts. If a sentence is performing intelligence, rewrite it.

Heath and Heath (*Made to Stick*): a message survives when it is simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and a story. The curse of knowledge makes the builder speak in abstractions the audience cannot hold. One concrete scene beats a framework diagram for memory.

Duarte (*DataStory*): a chart is not yet a claim. The audience needs to see what changed and what they are supposed to do. A dashboard dump fails Zinsser (noise) and Heath (no scene) and Duarte (no action) at once.

Jiwa (*Meaningful*): an idea spreads when it serves a story the audience already wants to be true about themselves. That is the marketing edge of Heath’s "emotional" and "story," and it is how a position becomes sayable. The position itself — the slot in a crowded mind — is Ries and Trout, in [Where to play](where-to-play.md).

Heath and Heath again, in *The Power of Moments*: people remember peaks, pits, and transitions, and a moment is built from elevation, insight, pride, and connection. Use it for a launch’s first session, for an onboarding pit you have been calling "activation," and for how you mark a team’s real win. Manufacturing peaks on top of a broken job is theater. Fix the job first.

For a product document, use the three writers as one pass, not three styles:

1. Duarte’s spine: what changed, what we will do.
2. Heath’s one concrete scene, so the abstraction has a body.
3. Zinsser’s cut: remove every sentence that does not serve those two.

## A hard week

1. Name peacetime or wartime. If wartime, pick one priority and say what will be worse on purpose.
2. If the work is early and fragile, run a Braintrust: candor, no authority taken from the owner.
3. If the work is a negotiation, write interests, options, criterion, and walkaway before the meeting. Use labels if the room is too hot for that conversation.
4. Rewrite the one-pager with the three-step pass above.
5. If the block is your own calendar, the attention limits are in [Judgment](judgment.md), and the one-on-one practice is in [How the team works](how-the-team-works.md).
