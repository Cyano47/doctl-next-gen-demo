# What to build

Use this when the backlog is already busy and it is still unclear whether anyone wants the thing. The books here agree on the sequence. They disagree on the artifact you put in front of a person, and on how often you do it.

**Paired with:** org design and roadmaps are in [How the team works](how-the-team-works.md). Whether a metric is real is in [Judgment](judgment.md). Which market deserves this work is in [Where to play](where-to-play.md).

## The sequence

1. **Name the situation, before the solution.** A person is trying to make progress in a circumstance. Klement (*When Coffee and Kale Compete*) treats that progress as the job: the push of the current situation, the pull of a new way, anxiety about the new way, and habit holding them in the old one. Christensen (*Competing Against Luck*) uses the job as the unit of analysis instead of the product category or the demographic. Coffee and kale compete when a person hires them for the same progress, even though no aisle in a store puts them side by side.
2. **Collect evidence from the life they have already lived.** Fitzpatrick (*The Mom Test*): talk about their life, ask about what they do now, and treat compliments, fluff, and ideas as worthless. Money, time, and a workaround they already use are the signals. "Would you use this?" ends the interview.
3. **Write the risk as a statement you can disprove.** Savoia (*Pretotype It*): make sure you are building the right *it* before you build it right. His form is a bet with a rate: at least this share of this group will do this costly thing. A pretotype asks for skin in the game (time, money, reputation, a real click on a real offer). An opinion does not count. Ries (*The Lean Startup*) then runs build–measure–learn on what is left. A startup, in his definition, is anyone creating something new under extreme uncertainty, including a team inside a large company. Vanity metrics feel like progress. Actionable metrics can change the plan. Pivot or persevere is a decision on the bet, not a mood.
4. **Fill the solution only after the underserved need is specific.** Olsen (*The Lean Product Playbook*) stacks product-market fit as a pyramid: target customer, underserved needs, value proposition, feature set, UX. Problem space and solution space stay separate. Customers are a good source of the problem and a poor source of the feature. Osterwalder’s value proposition canvas (jobs, pains, gains on one side; products, pain relievers, gain creators on the other) is a place to write that fit down. The business model canvas is the same move one level up: what has to be true around the product for the fit to be a business. Fill both from interviews and tests. A workshop with sticky notes and no customers produces a neat fiction.
5. **Test the smallest artifact that can falsify the bet.** Then decide. Torres (*Continuous Discovery Habits*) keeps a tree: outcome at the top, opportunities from customers in the middle, solutions at the leaves, assumption tests under the solution you are considering. She wants at least a weekly touch with customers, and a trio (product, design, engineering) looking at the same evidence. Productboard’s discovery playbook takes the same stance: discovery runs at the same time as delivery, and a framework is a small part of that. The rest is access to customers and a decision that changes because of what you heard.
6. **Check that the thing explains itself.** Norman (*The Design of Everyday Things*): people blame themselves for errors that the object caused. Affordances, signifiers, mapping, feedback, and a conceptual model the person can hold. If the control needs a tour to be understood, the control is wrong.

Adzic’s impact map is the bridge from this work into a backlog: goal, then the actor, then the change in their behavior, then the deliverable. A deliverable that changes no behavior does not go on the map. The planning workbook in the folder (concept, competitive scan, market research, MVP, release) is a later checklist. Run it after a pretotype has survived. Run it before that and it will manufacture a plan for a guess.

## Where they disagree

### What you are allowed to ask

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Fitzpatrick and Klement | You are still learning the situation. Ask for episodes: the last time they tried, what they paid, what they cobbled together. |
| Olsen and Ries | You have a specific offer to put in their hands. Watch what they do with it. |
| Christensen | You need the causal story of a switch they already made, not a reaction to your idea. |

A feature request is a solution in the customer’s words. Write down the struggle that produced it. Do not put the request on the roadmap because several people used the same words.

Lowdermilk and Rich (*The Customer-Driven Playbook*) show the failure mode in their opening: a team told to triple revenue, booking daily customer calls, and calling the activity "fail fast." Conversations without a hypothesis are a full calendar. Savoia and Ries both want a result that could embarrass the idea.

### MVP, pretotype, and prototype

These words get used as if they were one artifact.

| Word in these books | What it is | Follow it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pretotype (Savoia) | A test of demand that is cheaper than building. A door that does not open yet, a human hiding behind the product, a one-night offer. | You have not earned the right to write production code. |
| MVP (Ries) | The smallest thing that completes one turn of build–measure–learn, often in market, judged by an actionable metric. | The remaining risk is how people behave with a real offer, and you can name the number that would make you stop. |
| MVP (Olsen) | A test of the value proposition with customers, then another wave. | You are tuning fit, with a record of what changed between waves. |
| MVP (the planning workbook) | A phase between research and release. | You already know the problem and you are sequencing delivery. Using it before that is how teams skip steps 1–3. |
| Prototype (Cagan, *INSPIRED*) | A way to test value, usability, feasibility, or viability before a team commits to a product. | The learning does not require a shipped product. Cagan’s warning is that "MVP" becomes permission to ship something thin and call the damage learning. |

Cagan’s four risks belong in every test plan: will they buy or use it (value), can they figure it out (usability), can we build it (feasibility), does it work for the business (viability). Test the risk that could kill the idea. The org question — whether the team is allowed to run this before someone hands them a roadmap — is in [How the team works](how-the-team-works.md).

### Weekly discovery, or one intense week

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Torres, and Productboard’s playbook | The product is live and the tree of opportunities should get new evidence every week. Discovery is a habit, not a phase that ends at kickoff. |
| Knapp, Zeratsky, Kowitz (*Sprint*) | One hard question needs a decision now. Map the problem, sketch alone, decide, build a realistic facade, test with a handful of target users on the fifth day. |
| Ries | The loop should be as long as the bet requires. A five-day sprint is the wrong tool for a risk that is about a month of retention. A weekly interview is the wrong tool for a fork the whole team is stuck on. |

Do not relabel every sprint as discovery. Do not wait for a sprint to speak to a customer.

### Jobs, personas, and canvases

| Tool | What it is good for | Where it misleads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job and switch forces (Klement, Christensen) | Explains why a person fired the old way and hired a new one. Cuts across demographics. | Becomes a slogan on a slide ("the job is to save time") with no episode attached. |
| Persona and empathy map | A shared picture of a segment: what they say, do, think, feel. The empathy-map PDF in the folder had no extractable text, so this row is the usual map, not a quotation from that file. | The room invents an inner life. Klement’s attack on MBA segmentation is aimed at this. A persona is a summary of evidence you already gathered. |
| Value proposition and business model canvases (Osterwalder) | Force a fit between what you offer and what you claim they need, then a fit with the business around it. | The word "jobs" on the canvas is a box. It is not a switch interview. Filling it in a workshop feels like the Christensen work and is not. |
| Lean Product pyramid (Olsen) | Orders the work from customer to UX so the team cannot start at features. | "Target customer" slides back into a demographic if you skip the underserved need. |

When the canvas and the interviews disagree, the interviews win.

## A week you can run

1. Write one bet in Savoia’s shape: who, what costly action, what rate would count as real.
2. Do five Mom Test conversations about the last time that situation happened. No pitch.
3. Mark each story with Klement’s forces. If you cannot find a push, you do not have a job yet.
4. Pick the single riskiest of Cagan’s four risks. Choose a pretotype, a prototype, or an in-market MVP using the table above.
5. Set the disconfirming result before you show anyone anything. Duke’s rule for reading that result is in [Judgment](judgment.md): a failed test can be a noisy test, and a passed test can be luck. Name the result that would make you stop anyway.
